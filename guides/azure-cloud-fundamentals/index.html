00-03 minutes / The learning contract

Understand the cloud. Then understand Azure.

One story, twelve stops, sixty minutes. Learn to explain the system rather than memorize a catalogue of services.

You have just graduated. Your team wants to launch CampusCart, a fictional student marketplace. Ten people use it today; ten thousand might use it next term. It holds accounts, listings and photos. What should run where, who can change it, how do you pay, and what happens when something fails?

What you will be able to do

Draw Azure's hierarchy; trace a deployment and a user request; choose a service model and a region; distinguish access from policy; explain cost, resilience and responsibility; defend a simple architecture.

What an hour cannot do

Turn a beginner into a production expert. Expertise requires building, debugging and operating real systems. This hour builds the mental models that make that practice much faster. The course is not an exam certification or a claim of universal superiority.

Your method: read the model, apply it to CampusCart, answer the checkpoint without looking, then explain the answer aloud. The time budget includes these short checks. Optional deep links and the final glossary are reference material, not extra required reading.

Five questions organize the whole cloud: What runs? Where does it run? Who may act? What rules apply? How do we know it works and what it costs?
Before you begin: your baseline

Without searching, explain "a subscription", "a region" and "ARM". If you cannot, that is the intended starting point. Repeat this explanation at the end and compare how precisely you can connect the terms.

03-08 minutes / From a server to a service

Cloud is a way to obtain computing, not a place above the sky.

Cloud computing gives you on-demand access to shared computing services over a network: processing, storage, databases and more. A provider owns physical infrastructure; you provision what you need through a portal or an API. Cloud still uses real buildings, chips, disks, cables and people.

The old CampusCart plan

Buy servers for the busiest possible day. Wait for delivery. Install an operating system. Arrange power, networking, backups and spare parts. Pay even while idle. Too little capacity means rejected users; too much means unused equipment.

The cloud plan

Rent services now, adjust capacity, automate configuration and pay under the service's pricing model. You trade some physical control for speed and managed capabilities. You still own architecture, security decisions and spending.

PropertyMeaningDo not confuse it with
On demandProvision services when needed, usually through self-service APIs.Guaranteed access to every SKU. Quotas and capacity still apply.
ScalabilityHandle more work by scaling up a machine or out to more instances.Elasticity: automatically or dynamically matching changing demand.
Resource poolingThe provider shares fleets across customers with isolation.Customers being allowed to read one another's data.
Measured serviceUsage is metered, with usage, fixed-tier and commitment pricing.Everything being billed only while a request is running.

CapEx versus OpEx: buying equipment is typically capital expenditure; paying for service consumption is typically operating expenditure. This is a financing shift, not proof that cloud is always cheaper. Poorly sized always-on services can be expensive.

Public, private, hybrid, multicloud

Public cloud: provider-operated services offered to many customers. Private cloud: cloud-like infrastructure dedicated to one organization. Hybrid: on-premises/private systems connected with public cloud. Multicloud: using multiple cloud providers. Public cloud does not mean your application or data must be publicly accessible.

08-12 minutes / A platform, not a single product

Azure is Microsoft's cloud platform.

Azure offers infrastructure, application platforms, data services, AI and management tools. AWS and Google Cloud offer overlapping categories. There is no universal winner: compare the actual workload's reliability, cost, regional availability, skills, operational needs and regulatory constraints.

Why teams choose Azure

Integration with Microsoft identity and enterprise environments; managed application, data and AI services; hybrid management capabilities; a common governance layer. Azure also supports Linux, containers, open-source databases and non-Microsoft languages.

The trade-offs to check

Learning and operations complexity, provider-specific APIs, migration effort, egress costs, service limits and regional feature differences. Managed services reduce some work but also reduce some control.

12-17 minutes / Service models

IaaS, PaaS and SaaS describe how much you manage.

Think of food preparation: renting a kitchen, ordering a prepared meal, or subscribing to a meal service. The analogy helps with responsibility, but real contracts and service documentation define the boundaries.

ModelAzure / Microsoft exampleProvider operatesYou still operate
IaaS: infrastructure as a serviceAzure Virtual MachinesDatacenter, physical hosts and virtualizationGuest OS patching, applications, configuration, identities and data
PaaS: platform as a serviceAzure App Service, Azure SQL DatabaseInfrastructure and the managed runtime/database platformYour code/schema, supported configuration, access, data protection and application behavior
SaaS: software as a serviceMicrosoft 365The application and underlying platformTenant configuration, identities, devices, access and data governance

Serverless means you do not manage server instances in the traditional way; it does not mean no servers exist. Azure Functions and event-driven platforms may scale on demand, but plans differ in billing, cold starts, limits and always-on behavior. Serverless is not automatically free.

Shared responsibility never disappears. You remain responsible for your data, identities, access decisions and appropriate configuration. A provider's security certification does not certify your application.

CampusCart choice: use a managed web platform and managed database unless you have a requirement that justifies VM-level control. "We know how to install an OS" is not by itself a reason to accept patching and recovery work.

17-24 minutes / Identity and governance

Where resources belong is not where their servers live.

Entra tenantIdentity directory / Trust boundary
Management groupsGovernance across subscriptions
SubscriptionBilling, access and service-limit boundary
Resource groupRelated lifecycle / Management container
ResourceAn instance of a service

Tenant: an organization's Microsoft Entra ID directory holds identities, applications and groups. A subscription trusts one Entra tenant at a time; a tenant can be associated with many subscriptions. A person's account can be granted access across multiple tenants. Identity roles and Azure resource roles are different systems.

Management group: a governance scope above subscriptions. Put CampusCart's development and production subscriptions under a university management group to apply shared policies and role assignments. Groups may nest. Each child management group or subscription has one parent. Descendant subscriptions trust the same tenant. A management group is not a server, a region or a billing invoice.

Subscription: where resource consumption is associated with billing and where many quotas and permissions are scoped. Separate development from production for stronger boundaries, not merely different tags. Billing accounts and billing profiles are commercial structures; they are not the same hierarchy as management groups.

Resource group: an ARM container for related resources you manage together. Most everyday service resources belong to one resource group. It is not a network or a datacenter. Resources in it can be in different regions and can connect to other groups. Deleting the group deletes the resources in it, subject to locks and service behavior.

Resource: one managed instance, such as a storage account, virtual network or database server. A provider and resource type identify what it is; its resource ID identifies this particular instance.

Inheritance: what flows down, and what does not?

Role assignments and policy assignments can apply to descendant scopes. A subscription Owner cannot simply bypass an inherited deny policy. Resource-group tags are not automatically inherited by resources. Some administrative resources exist at subscription, management-group or tenant scope rather than inside a resource group. The tenant is the identity context, not an ordinary fifth Azure RBAC resource scope.

24-29 minutes / What happens when you click Create?

Azure Resource Manager is the management front door.

Portal / CLI / SDKYou request a resource change
ARMAuthentication, authorization and governance
Resource providerService-specific management API
ResourceThe requested instance is created or updated

ARM stands for Azure Resource Manager, the deployment and management service. It is not the Arm CPU architecture. Creating a storage account from the portal, Azure CLI or a Bicep deployment uses the same management platform. Providers such as Microsoft.Storage and Microsoft.Compute implement service-specific resource operations.

ARM template means a JSON infrastructure definition. Bicep is a more concise authoring language that compiles into ARM templates. Declarative infrastructure says what should exist, rather than listing every imperative step. APIs are versioned: the version selects a contract, not a newer physical server.

Control plane

Create a database server, resize a VM, configure a network or assign access. This is resource management through ARM. The Azure Activity Log records management events.

Data plane

Read a student photo, run SQL or send a queue message. These requests usually go directly to service endpoints, not through ARM. They need service-specific authentication, authorization and network connectivity.

/subscriptions/{subscription-id}/resourceGroups/rg-campuscart-dev
/providers/Microsoft.Storage/storageAccounts/{account-name}

Read that ID as: which subscription / which group / which provider and type / which instance. Its components form one continuous path; the line break above is only for readability.

Common failure: Contributor access can let you manage a storage account without granting permission to read its blobs. Management access and data access must both be designed. An ARM deployment succeeding does not prove the application's endpoint works.

29-35 minutes / Azure's physical map

Governance is logical. Location is physical.

GeographyA defined data-residency boundary containing regions
RegionA named area containing datacenters and networking
Availability zoneAn isolated datacenter group within a region
DatacenterPhysical buildings, hardware and facilities

Geography: a boundary such as Europe or the United States used in Azure's global infrastructure model. It may contain multiple regions. Choosing a geography helps address residency requirements; verify the specific service's replication, backup and processing commitments rather than assuming the label guarantees everything.

Region: an area such as West Europe where you deploy regional services. The portal's display name may differ from the API location name, for example West Europe versus westeurope. Some services are global/nonregional. Available products, versions, SKUs, prices and quotas differ by region.

Availability zone: a physically separated group of one or more datacenters with independent power, cooling and networking. Zones isolate many local failures. Not every region or service supports zones. A service being available in a zonal region does not mean your configuration is automatically zone-redundant.

Resource-group location: where the group's management metadata is stored. It does not force every resource into that region. A subscription is not tied to one region either.

Region pairs and sovereign clouds: two misconceptions

Some Azure regions are paired; many newer regions are not. A pair is not automatic application replication or failover. You must configure and test the relevant service behavior. Azure Government and Azure operated by 21Vianet are separate cloud environments with different endpoints, eligibility and feature availability; they are not merely another region in the public cloud.

CampusCart region decision: first meet residency obligations, then verify service/SKU availability and capacity, then consider user latency, resilience and cost. The closest region is not always an eligible one. Cross-region copies and transfers have operational and commercial consequences.

40-45 minutes / Three separate gates

A request needs an identity, permission and a path.

Identity and access

Authentication: who are you? Authorization: what may you do? Azure RBAC combines a security principal, role and scope. Use least privilege. A managed identity lets an Azure workload obtain tokens without storing a password in its code.

Network reachability

A virtual network is a private IP network, not a resource group. Subnets divide it. Network security groups filter traffic. Private endpoints provide private IP access to supported services. DNS resolves names to addresses. Correct RBAC cannot fix incorrect DNS or a blocked network path.

MechanismQuestion answeredExample
RBACWho may perform which action at which scope?Developer gets Reader in production
Azure PolicyIs this resource configuration allowed or compliant?Deny disallowed deployment locations
Resource lockShould certain control-plane modifications be blocked?CanNotDelete lock on a production group
TagsHow do we label and attribute resources?owner=campus-team, environment=prod

Policy effects differ: audit reports, deny blocks applicable requests, and modify/deployIfNotExists have specific enforcement and remediation behavior. A lock is not a backup and does not generally prevent data-plane deletion. Tags are not a security boundary and do not automatically inherit from a resource group.

CampusCart request: the web app's managed identity obtains a token; the database authorizes it; DNS and network rules let it reach the endpoint. Student-facing authorization still needs code that checks which student owns which listing.

45-50 minutes / Operate, do not just deploy

Healthy infrastructure is not the same as a healthy application.

High availability reduces downtime from expected failures. Disaster recovery restores service after larger failures. Backup keeps recoverable copies. Replication can copy an accidental deletion; backups need tested restoration. None of these is a synonym for another.

Define recovery in numbers

RTO: target time to restore service. RPO: target maximum acceptable data loss measured in time. "RTO 1 hour, RPO 15 minutes" means restore within an hour and tolerate at most about fifteen minutes of lost changes. These are objectives you design and test, not defaults Azure promises for every app.

Define what users see

SLI: measured behavior, such as successful requests. SLO: your reliability target. SLA: a provider's contractual service commitment and remedies under stated conditions. Several dependencies can reduce end-to-end reliability; do not assume one service's SLA is your whole application's SLA.

Observe three layers: metrics show quantities and trends; logs explain events; distributed traces follow requests through dependencies. Activity Log helps with management changes; Application Insights helps with application telemetry. Service Health reports Azure incidents affecting your services; Resource Health reports individual resource state.

Cost equation: service tier/capacity + storage + requests + network transfer + supporting services + operational effort. A budget usually alerts; it is not a hard spending cap. Reservations and savings plans trade commitment for qualifying discounts and require thoughtful utilization planning.

Quota versus capacity: quota is your allowed amount; capacity is what Azure can actually allocate. Permission, available quota and an offered SKU do not guarantee a successful allocation.

Why stopping a VM might not stop its bill

Stopping inside the guest may leave compute allocated and billed. Deallocation removes the ordinary allocated-compute charge, but disks, certain IP resources, backups and other services can still incur charges. Always verify resource state and the actual cost breakdown.

Wrap the application in two logical views: governance (tenant, management group, subscriptions, groups, RBAC, policy) and operations (region choices, recovery, telemetry, budget, deployment automation).

Your two-minute design defense

  1. Name which identities deploy the app and which identities read data.
  2. Place development and production in the hierarchy and explain the boundary.
  3. Select a service model and justify the work you are delegating.
  4. Explain region eligibility and why zones are not automatic regional disaster recovery.
  5. Describe a deployment through ARM and a student request through the data plane.
  6. State RTO/RPO, a backup/restore test, an error alert and a cost owner.
Compare with an example answer after you explain yours

Use separate dev/prod subscriptions under a university management group. Apply approved-location policies at the appropriate parent scope, and least-privilege production roles. Group resources by lifecycle, not by arbitrary service category. Start with App Service, a managed relational database and Blob Storage where they satisfy requirements. Choose an eligible European region after verifying service behavior, residency and availability.

Use federated deployment identities or managed identities where supported; give the application only necessary data permissions. Restrict data endpoints as required and configure DNS. Bicep describes resource configuration; deployments use ARM; user requests use application/service endpoints. Define recovery targets, enable supported backups, test restoration, monitor application failure rates and assign budget alerts to a responsible owner. Zone/multiregion choices depend on recovery requirements and supported configurations, not just a map.
